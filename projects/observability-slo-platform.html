Observability and SLO platform

The problem

There was no shortage of alerts. That was the problem. On-call had learned which ones to ignore, which meant the signal that mattered arrived in the same channel as the noise and got the same treatment.

Underneath that was a correlation problem. Metrics lived with one vendor, logs with another, and traces barely existed. Answering "is this slow because of the database or because of the network" meant three tabs and a guess.

Constraints

  • Two clouds. A single vendor agent that only understands one of them was not an option.
  • Cardinality is the cost driver. One well-meaning label with a user ID in it can multiply the bill.
  • Correlation has to be structural, not something an engineer does by eye across timestamps.
  • Alert volume has to fall, not just get re-routed.
  • Backends must be replaceable. I did not want the instrumentation to be a lock-in decision.

Architecture

Everything emits to an OpenTelemetry Collector, and only to the collector. Workloads on AKS and EKS, exporters in front of managed services, and synthetic probes running real user journeys all speak one wire format. The collector applies tail sampling and cardinality caps before anything reaches a backend.

From there it fans out: Prometheus for metrics, Loki for structured logs, Tempo for traces. Grafana joins all three, linked by trace ID, so a spike on a graph leads to the trace and the trace leads to the log line. SLO burn rates over a one hour and a six hour window drive Alertmanager, which routes by ownership: fast burn pages a human, slow burn opens a ticket.

Decisions and trade-offs

Every one of these had a cost. Listing the cost is the point.

DecisionWhyWhat it costs
The collector is the only ingress pathOne place to enforce sampling, cardinality limits and redaction. Swapping a backend becomes a configuration change rather than a re-instrumentation project.The collector becomes a single point of failure, so it runs as a DaemonSet feeding a gateway tier rather than as one deployment.
Burn-rate alerting instead of static thresholdsPages fire when the error budget is genuinely threatened, which is the only condition worth waking someone for.Useless until SLOs exist, and defining an SLO that the service owner actually agrees with is the hard part of the whole project.
Tail sampling rather than head samplingThe decision is made after the trace completes, so slow and failed traces are kept and the boring successful ones are dropped.Requires buffering complete traces in the collector, which costs memory and puts a ceiling on trace duration.
Cardinality caps enforced at the collector, with drops loggedCost stays bounded regardless of what a service decides to label.Silently dropping a label is worse than the cost it saves, so every drop is logged and surfaced. Otherwise a dashboard quietly goes wrong.
No runbook, no pageIf nobody can write down what to do about an alert, it is not actionable, and paging on it only trains people to ignore pages.Some genuinely urgent conditions go un-paged until someone writes the runbook. That pressure is the point.

What it achieves

Measured as design targets for the build:

  • One query language and one pane covers both clouds. There is no per-cloud dashboard split.
  • A latency spike leads to its trace and then to its logs by trace ID, without correlating timestamps by hand.
  • Every paging alert has a runbook link, enforced by a check on the alert rules themselves.
  • Ingest cost is bounded by collector policy rather than by how carefully each service names its labels.
  • Changing a storage backend does not require touching application instrumentation.

What I would change

I built the pipeline before the SLOs, which meant the alerting layer sat unused for longer than it should have while I chased service owners for agreement on what "healthy" meant.

The instrumentation work felt like progress and the SLO conversations felt like meetings, so I did the instrumentation first. That was the wrong order. The conversation is the project; the pipeline is just what makes the conversation enforceable.
