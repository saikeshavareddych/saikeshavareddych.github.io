Hackathon platform, 48 hours

Forty teams, isolated namespaces, automatic subdomains and TLS. Built to stand up in minutes under a hard deadline and destroy itself on schedule.

The problem

Forty teams need somewhere to deploy, reachable from the internet, isolated from each other, provisioned faster than they lose patience, and gone before the bill becomes a conversation. The whole thing exists for forty-eight hours.

Everything about this build is shaped by the deadline. There is no second attempt, no maintenance window, and no opportunity to fix it properly later, because there is no later.

Constraints

  • Fixed budget, hard stop. Spend has to end when the event ends, not when someone remembers to clean up.
  • Participants get no cluster-level access. Forty strangers with cluster-admin is not isolation.
  • DNS and TLS must be automatic. Nobody is issuing forty certificates by hand at 2am.
  • Provisioning measured in minutes. A team waiting on infrastructure is a team not building.
  • Failure of one team's workload cannot affect another's.

Architecture

A signup form feeds a queue. A Terraform provisioner drains it, creating one namespace per team with a resource quota, a default-deny network policy and the restricted pod security standard applied. Each namespace gets a DNS record and an ingress on its own subdomain automatically, covered by a single wildcard certificate issued over DNS-01.

Shared services sit alongside: a pull-through registry cache, CI runners that scale to zero, and a read-only scoreboard replica so that judging traffic cannot take down the primary. The teardown job is written and scheduled before the event starts, not after.

Decisions and trade-offs

Every one of these had a cost. Listing the cost is the point.

DecisionWhyWhat it costs
One shared cluster with a namespace per team, not a cluster per teamProvisioning drops from minutes per team to seconds per team, and the cost is a fraction of forty control planes.Weaker isolation than separate clusters. Mitigated with quotas, default-deny network policy and restricted pod security, but a kernel-level escape would cross the boundary and I accepted that risk for a 48-hour event with known participants.
A single wildcard certificate over DNS-01, not per-team certificatesForty certificate requests in ten minutes runs straight into rate limits, at the worst possible moment.One private key covering every subdomain is a larger blast radius. For a temporary event that trade was clearly worth it; for a permanent multi-tenant platform it would not be.
Pull-through registry cache in front of the upstream registryForty teams pulling the same base images at the same moment is the actual bottleneck at T-0, not compute.One more component to run and monitor during the event, with no time to debug it if it misbehaves.
The teardown job is committed before the event opensSpend stops on schedule whether or not anyone remembers. The destroy path is tested while there is still time to fix it.A scheduled destroy is dangerous if the event runs over. It is guarded by a one-time extension flag that has to be set deliberately, rather than by disabling the job.
Runners autoscale to zero between buildsMost of forty-eight hours is teams writing code, not building. Idle capacity is the easiest cost to remove.First build after a quiet period pays a cold-start penalty, which is noticeable and mildly annoying at exactly the wrong time.

What it achieves

Measured as design targets for the build:

  • Cluster and forty namespaces reach ready in roughly eleven minutes from an empty state.
  • Each team gets a working HTTPS subdomain without filing a request or waiting on a human.
  • The judging traffic spike is absorbed by autoscaling rather than by someone watching a dashboard.
  • Everything is destroyed on schedule and spend stops, without depending on anyone remembering.
  • One team's crash loop or resource hog cannot reach another team's namespace.

What I would change

The first ten minutes were almost entirely image pulls. I had built the registry cache but had not warmed it, so the cache spent T-0 doing exactly the work it existed to avoid, while forty teams watched pods stay in ContainerCreating.

Pre-pulling the base images onto every node before the event opens is a five-line change and would have removed most of that window. It is the clearest example I have of a design being right and the operational detail being wrong, which under a deadline amounts to the same thing.
